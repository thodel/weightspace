# Review of "Reading and editing the weights of text recognition models: what a language model can learn from a family of kraken models" (draft of 7 November 2026)

Reviewer for JDMDH. Files read: paper/manuscript.md, docs/RELATED_WORK.md (head), docs/RESULTS.md, docs/PHASE5_REPORT.md, docs/HERTWIG_REVISIONS.md, docs/PAPER.md, PLAN.md (head), the preregistration and notes of session 1 under runs/. paper/STATUS.md does not exist although the draft cites it, and docs/BRIEF_ZOO_SURVEY.md, cited in section 6.5, does not exist either. I did not re-run any computation and did not verify the bibliography entries (all cited keys exist in references.bib; several 2026 references and the blog post could not be checked).

## 1. Summary

The manuscript applies the "weight-space programming" idea of a November 2026 blog post (a language model reads a network's weights, proposes edits, tests them with a few forward passes) to seven in-house kraken recognisers (CNN + 3 BiLSTM + CTC, 4.1 M parameters). It (a) reads parent/child weight differences for five lineage pairs, (b) measures per-block interpolation between a Kurrent parent and child, (c) reports two budgeted single-shot editing sessions by two different Claude models (7 and 8 edits, 3 kept, none improving held-out CER beyond noise), (d) reports a lossless removal of 11 M gradient-free weights from a kraken+ head, and (e) documents two data defects (a dataset export with swapped images; a printed book in a handwriting test set). It concludes that weight reading is cheap and descriptive, that editing did not help, and that the real value is the evaluation discipline.

The honest, carefully logged reporting is the paper's strongest asset, and the data-defect forensics are useful. But as it stands the manuscript is a lab report of a pilot, not yet an article. The research question that motivates it (can an LLM working with weights do something useful for ATR, and what does that say about LLMs) is only weakly tested: there is no positive control, the programmer is a one-shot code generator working from summary tables written by the authors, the practice sets are the models' own training lines, the key controls are unrun, and the model-zoo comparison and the adapter track (the two parts that would have given breadth and a link to LLM internals) are missing. The text also contradicts itself in several places (listed in section 3). The humanities contribution is not articulated.

Recommendation: major revision. A negative result is publishable in JDMDH if it is framed as an audit with a positive control and the zoo comparison is delivered.

## 3. Major concerns

### 3.1 No positive control: the negative result is not interpretable
The study concludes that weight reading "did not locate a repairable defect in any model we examined". But the method has never been shown to find a defect that is known to exist. Every model examined was healthy (family 2), had a data defect that is not visible in weights (family 1), or was an architectural redundancy (the rewrite). The blog post's own claim is that edits "remove an obstacle". The cheapest and most informative missing experiment is a planted-defect test: take `german-xix-blb-v1` and corrupt it in known ways (zero or negate a row; scale a layer; add noise to one LSTM; swap two character rows; add a bias offset to blank), then run the same protocol and see whether the programmer detects and repairs it. If it cannot, the negative result is about the method's power and says little about ATR models; if it can, the "healthy models have nothing to repair" reading is supported. CPU only, no training, a day of work.

### 3.2 The practice set is the model's own training data, so the protocol cannot find generalising edits
Practice sets are "300 lines cut from the training side of the data". Fine-tuned models read their own training lines far better than anything else (Table 2: 0.065 on practice against 0.096 on the corrected held-out; Bern 0.083 vs 0.19-0.27). Edits are kept by their gain on lines the network has fitted, so the keep signal measures fit, not generalisation. Section 7.4 discovers the donor consequence (a transplant from the parent "looks good" on practice because the parent trained on those lines) and calls it a weakness "corrected for the future", but the reported sessions are not corrected. The programmer's reasoning in Session 1 ("the frames where a space is dropped are frames where the blank wins against everything") is also formed on lines the model has memorised. A fair test needs a practice set drawn from a validation split that is disjoint from training and from the held-out set. This is cheap (the data exist: the Bern and BLB projects have test/eval splits; carve a validation part from the held-out side or from unused pages) and the sessions are cheap (173 s of CPU for eight passes). I would not accept the RQ2 conclusion until at least one session per family is repeated this way.

### 3.3 The "LLM as programmer" is a one-shot code generator, which is not the method under test
Section 3.2 says the programmer writes "the whole program in advance" and the sandbox "runs it unattended", with the cost that "the programmer cannot react to a result within a session". The blog's whole claim concerns an agent that interleaves edits and measurements. A session that cannot use the budget adaptively tests "LLM writes a list of edits", not weight-space programming, and the budget of ten forward passes is largely meaningless (the program is fixed regardless of the result). Yet RQ2 and the conclusion generalise to "no edit that a language model proposed improved a model". Moreover the text contradicts the design: section 7.3 says "The programmer left two passes unspent, because every lever had been measured at two sizes and a third would have fitted the practice set", which reads as an in-loop decision, while the run notes (runs/20261106-german-xix-blb-v1-b10-1/notes.md) state that the plan was in `program.py` and the notes were written after the run. The reason for the unspent passes is a post hoc explanation, not a decision. Also, in Session 1 only passes 1-8 of 10 were run because the program had eight hypotheses (H8 was conditional and never triggered), not because the programmer chose to stop.
Related: the programmer saw only tables of per-tensor statistics, the practice set, and the confusion lists. The hypotheses (blank bias, space row, rare rows) are the ones any CTC practitioner would try first (blank-prior/blank-penalty tuning is a standard decoding trick). So the contribution of "the LLM" to the hypotheses is untested; a blind programmer (C6) and a hand-written list of the same edits by a human or a simple coordinate search would give the comparison. These controls are defined in the protocol (docs/PROTOCOL.md) and not run.

### 3.4 Two sessions, two different programmers, a changed keep rule, different targets: nothing can be attributed
Session 1 (Claude Fable 5.1, old keep rule, Kurrent model, 7 edits, 2 kept) and Session 2 (Claude Opus 5.5, new keep rule, Bern model, 8 edits, 1 kept, with the first session's report at hand) differ in model, rule, target, data and context. The paper itself admits it "cannot say whether better calibration reflects the model, the experience or the nature of the target". With n = 1 per cell no statement about "a language model" is possible, only about two particular runs. The counts "15 edits, 3 kept" in the abstract mix two keep rules; under the current rule Session 1 would have kept nothing, but that is a counterfactual (the programmer might have chosen differently). Prompts, system prompts, sampling settings and the full preregistration/program files must be released; a reviewer will also ask for repeated runs (e.g. 5 sessions per model with different seeds) and for at least one non-Anthropic model, since the authors also use the same model family for drafting, literature search and code.

### 3.5 Statistics are too optimistic and not what they are called
- Lines are resampled independently, but lines are clustered in pages and objects (Session 2 held-out: 545 lines from 14 pages; Session 1: 5,628 lines from 33 objects, half from one printed book). Intervals such as [-0.048, +0.026] on 5,628 lines are far too narrow. Use a cluster (page/object-level) bootstrap or mixed model; it will widen intervals and will not change the conclusion (which is already "no gain"), but it changes the claim in Table 8 that the Schlussredaktion loss (+0.51 [+0.21, +0.82]) is "significant" and the practice-set claims in Table 4.
- Sequential keep rule with eight tests per session and no multiplicity control: Session 2's single "kept" edit has interval [-0.85, -0.04], i.e. borderline, and the kept edit is exactly the one that exploits the memorised lines. The expected number of false keeps under an interval test at 95 % is non-trivial over 15 edits.
- "Preregistered" is overstated: preregistration is a file in the authors' own private repository, written by the programmer model, with no external time stamp. Call it "pre-specified and logged", or deposit the preregistration files (OSF, Zenodo) before the next sessions.
- Table 7's "Predicted" column holds "refused" for six of eight hypotheses, which is a decision, not a predicted effect size; the claim of "seven correct predictions" in 8.1 is therefore weaker than it reads.
- Section 8.4 says predicted sizes were "one to two orders of magnitude too large". Predicted 0.1-1.0 points against measured 0.016-0.05 is a factor of 5-50, and when the true effect is zero a ratio is not meaningful. Report predicted versus measured as a table/plot with the practice noise level (the interval widths are 0.3-0.6 points, larger than most predicted effects).
- The α-sweep (Table 4) gives intervals on the practice set (training data) only, and the whole sweep is on one model pair.

### 3.6 Overclaim: "the gain of a fine-tune is held jointly by all blocks" and "interactions dominate"
The evidence is eight single-block halvings costing 0.0-2.3 points, summing to about 6.3 points, against a total gap of 23.9 points. The draft reads this as joint dependence. But if the error were an additive quadratic function of the distance moved, halving a block's move would cost one quarter of that block's contribution, and the total of the eight halvings would be one quarter of the gap: 0.25 x 23.9 = 6.0, against the observed 6.3. The table is therefore what an additive, interaction-free quadratic model predicts, not evidence for interactions. Statements in 7.2, 8.4 and 9.1(2) ("gains are not additive across blocks, and interactions dominate") are not supported. The experiments that would test it are cheap: interpolate all blocks together (global α sweep, also called linear mode connectivity), pairs of blocks, and compare the sum of single-block effects with the joint effect at the same α. The asymmetry at α = 1.5 (LSTM costs fall from 1.9 to 0.3 points) is the more interesting observation and is under-discussed. In the same section "the last LSTM block is where this model learnt Kurrent spacing" is inferred from the space-deletion count after halving LSTM 3 only; the same breakdown for LSTM 1 and 2 (which cost 1.9 and 1.2 points) is not given, so the localisation claim is not shown. Finally, the claim that a large relative change is not a large functional change (second conv bias) is partly an artefact of using relative L2 on a bias vector whose norm is small.

### 3.7 Part 9 as the authors imagine it is not supported by these experiments (see also the section below)
The manuscript is honest here (9.3). My concern is the mismatch between title/abstract/narrative and content, and a hidden issue in 9.2: its list of what "the language model" did well rests on two single-shot programs.

### 3.8 The reading results (section 6.3) are descriptive without a baseline
"Fine-tuning leaves consistent traces" is shown by rows of rare characters growing and the blank bias rising. Needed: (a) a null: how big are these changes between two runs of the same fine-tune with different seeds (the `hertwig-v1` to `hertwig-v2` pair is a first approximation: blank +0.002, mean row change 0.19, i.e. almost as large as for the true fine-tunes, which weakens the claim that the row change is a trace of fine-tuning rather than of any training); (b) a statement that softmax is invariant to adding the same constant to all biases, so the blank bias is only meaningful relative to the other rows; the readings should report blank minus mean of other biases; (c) honesty about n: four fine-tune pairs, two families, non-independent; (d) the mechanism offered ("a class that only receives a negative gradient is pushed away from every input") is the standard explanation but is not tested here. The text calls the third observation (conv moves less than recurrent) one that "holds across all five pairs", yet Table 3 reports the largest block change for hertwig-v2 to schlussredaktion-v1 as the convolutional stack (0.07-0.16) and Table 4 shows relative changes for the Kurrent pair of 0.26-0.30 in conv 3-4 against 0.26-0.27 in the LSTMs and 0.195 in the output layer. The observation does not hold across the pairs as stated; the text then half-acknowledges this with "one exception" and "the Bern fine-tunes moved the recurrent stack and the classifier instead", which Table 3 contradicts for one of the Bern pairs.

### 3.9 Reproducibility and data availability fail the journal's open-data expectations
The repository is private, both datasets are private, the model weights sit on private storage, and the published numbers depend on dataset revisions that were repaired by hand (commit `dda578ea`). A reader cannot re-run anything. JDMDH will ask for a persistent archive (Zenodo/Software Heritage) of code, preregistrations, logs, sparse diffs, line lists with hashes, and at minimum the six model files (16 MB each, easily deposited) plus the Bern data (sixteenth-century manuscripts; consider whether rights are an issue) or a derived release. The harness is also developed with several overlapping data fixes after the first session (held-out redefined, collapse detector changed), so a frozen, tagged release is needed.

### 3.10 Significance for the digital humanities is not argued
The paper never says what a project historian or editor gains. The one concrete humanities-relevant result, that a dataset export silently paired training pages with other pages' images and that a "handwriting" test set contained a printed book with OCR ground truth, is a data-curation finding, and it came from conventional QA (a kraken test failure on a training page; sorting errors by size and looking at them), not from weight reading. The conclusion that "the main practical value lies in the protocol it forces" is therefore not demonstrated: no counterfactual shows that a project without the protocol would have missed the defects. The paper should either (a) reframe around the data-defect findings and the evaluation hygiene lessons with the weight work as the setting, or (b) deliver the zoo comparison as a descriptive contribution to the field ("what do the models we all share look like inside?"), which has an obvious readership.

### 3.11 The rewrite is not a result of the method
Section 7.5 and contribution 4 present the removal of 11,054,080 gradient-free weights as the one edit that "works as intended". It is a sound engineering fix, but it (i) is an architectural redundancy (a 255-row kernel over a height-1 map with padding) that can be found from the VGSL string without reading any weight; (ii) was not found or proposed by the LLM, as far as the manuscript says; (iii) concerns a model that is a collapsed training run and a architecture ("kraken+") that is not part of the seven-model family and is described as dropped in section 2.2 and "not needed for anything that is served" in 7.5; (iv) is described as "an architecture we had used in an earlier project" and so is a lesson about the authors' own earlier design. The padding-row-norm observation (rows at initialisation norm) is a nice illustration that weights never trained stay at initialisation, but it does not belong in the list of contributions or in 9.1(5) as an insight about recognisers. Also: section 2.1 says all seven models share one specification and section 6.1 says all seven have 4.11 M parameters, which is false for this seventh model (15.2 M, or 4.15 M after rewrite).

## 4. Further concerns, by theme

### 4.1 Claims versus evidence and internal contradictions (checked against the text)
1. Number of models: "seven in-house models" (abstract, 2.1, 2.2, 6.1) but Table 1 lists six and the seventh is a kraken+ architecture that is said to share the specification of the others (it does not), and that family is described as "dropped" in 2.2.
2. Cross-references: Table 1 caption refers to "Sections 6.3 and 7.5" for comparability of reported CERs (should be 6.4 and probably 7.6); the table row for combined-v1 says "see Section 6.3" (6.4); section 2.2 refers to section 6.4 for the dropped family 3 (6.4 is about combined-v1).
3. Session 1 "proposed seven edits" and "registered eight hypotheses"; Table 5 shows seven; H8 was conditional. Say so. Section 3.2 says ten passes budgeted but "passes 9-10 unspent" is as discussed.
4. Section 6.4 says every model read the Hertwig practice lines with a CER of 0.76-0.86, and Table 2 says 0.08-0.21 for the same lines. Both are true only because of the image swap (25 October vs 22 October export), but the reader of Table 2 is not told which export it uses. The table needs the export/revision identifier, and 6.4 should be placed before Table 2 or Table 2 flagged.
5. 7.6: "With the 221 degenerate, vertical and upside-down lines re-cut, the held-out CER is 9.55 percent" and "On `blb-eval-hand` ... CER of 9.55 percent". Two different sets and two different procedures give the identical figure to two decimals; the same number appears for the "after" reading. Please check that this is not a copy error. Also "the 12.34 percent of the first measurement and the trainer's own 8.7 percent are explained by the differences" is not an explanation; it is asserted. The 8.7 and 8.07 are on different sets with different line selections.
6. The held-out set for Session 1 was redefined after seeing Session 1's results (section 7.6), and the original conclusion is then confirmed on the redefined set. The paper notes this in the limits but states the "conclusion does not depend on it"; this is true for a null result, but readers should be told that the re-aggregation of the same reading is not independent evidence (as the text partly does).
7. 6.4: "the label noise cost it about three points on the Hertwig hand" compares the child with the parent on the Hertwig test (26.7 vs 23.6), but the child was also trained on 118 extra Schlussredaktion pages, so the three points are confounded; the clean comparator (retraining on the corrected data) is exactly the missing control C4. "Combined-v1 is the best of the three overall" is in the same paragraph a surprising result for a model trained on 44/50 wrong-image Hertwig pages and deserves a sentence of analysis (the Hertwig part is only 1,786 of 5,441 lines).
8. 8.2: "a bias reading of the output layer cannot see that the margins at the deleted frames are large" is a mechanistic claim without a measurement; nobody computed the blank-versus-character margins at the deleted frames. 
9. 9.1(1): "mostly cosmetic" is a rhetorical term; the output-layer α = 0.5 sweep costs 0.23 points on the Kurrent model, small but not zero and significant.
10. 9.1(6) generalises from one model with an unusually severe and atypical corruption (swapped images) to "training on label noise".
11. 7.6: "classified every one of them. None was a readable handwritten line..." Who classified (authors, LLM), by which rules, with what agreement? The classification of 428 lines is a manual analysis in the core of the argument.
12. The abstract says "two sessions ... proposed fifteen edits; the three that were kept did not lower held-out error beyond noise". Session 2's kept edit raised held-out error (+0.15, interval including zero; Schlussredaktion significantly worse by the authors' own interval). State that.
13. Abstract and conclusion: "positive for the discipline of preregistered, interval-checked, held-out evaluation" is a conclusion not tested by any comparison.

### 4.2 Clarity for a humanities readership
- Jargon without explanation at first use: CTC, blank symbol, LSTM, codec, VGSL, forward pass, logit, bias, relative L2, cosine, bootstrap, paired interval, held-out, practice set, collapse, deletion/insertion/substitution, donor, transplant, sandbox, "kept/reverted". Add a one-page box or glossary and one schematic figure of the recogniser (the existing architecture figure is rectangles by tensor).
- BLB, the Laßberg papers and the Bern volumes are `[TODO]`; the sources, scripts, centuries and sizes of the data are not described in a way a reader can interpret the CER values (what is the CER on printed vs handwritten, on which hands?). "Hertwig", "Schlussredaktion" are not explained.
- The numerical style: a mix of "0.083", "8.3 %", "points", "percent" for CER; use percent throughout.
- The manuscript spends many words on forensic detail (export hash story, shelfmark spelling, collapse classes) that interrupts the argument; move to an appendix or supplementary file.
- Sections 6 and 7 mix methods, results and discussion. The title and abstract do not reflect the paper's actual main finding.

### 4.3 Length and structure
About 9,500 words, 8 tables and 8 figures for a mostly negative result. A cut to roughly 6,000-7,000 words is feasible: shorten 7.3-7.4 (keep one table per session in the main text, rest in an appendix), move 6.4 and 7.6 to an appendix or a separate short data-quality note, drop the rewrite from the contributions. Keep one clean results table. The abstract has two paragraphs and a TODO; reduce to one. A `[TODO]` count of 11 (including competing interests and funding) indicates the draft is not submission-ready.

### 4.4 Related work
- Good and honest. The documented search is a model for the field. But: (a) say explicitly who read the 58 full texts; (b) literature missing next to the weight work: linear mode connectivity and interpolation of fine-tuned networks (Frankle et al.; Entezari et al.; Neyshabur et al.), model zoos as a data object (Schürholt et al., "model zoos" and weight-space learning/hyper-representations), analyses of fine-tuning updates (e.g. low-rank/intrinsic-dimension studies), logit adjustment/blank-prior correction in CTC decoding (the very edit tried in H1-H2), calibration of CTC posteriors; (c) work on LLM agents doing model analysis/repair/AutoML and interpretability agents (automated interpretability, agents that propose and test hypotheses about networks) which is the correct frame for "an LLM as programmer"; (d) the DH literature on model sharing and model cards beyond Romein et al., and on HTR evaluation (CER confidence intervals, test-set contamination) to position the data-defect findings; (e) the 2026 references (the blog post, several 2026 papers) should be double checked and archived.
- The blog is cited as a "recent LLM paper": it is a blog post by four authors, not peer reviewed; the manuscript should say so, quote its limited scope, and archive it (Wayback or Zenodo).
- "In object" (section 5.2, "closest study to ours in object") should read "in subject" or "in spirit".

### 4.5 LLM-use and ethics
- Disclosure should cover the model versions and dates, prompts, which sections were drafted by an LLM, and which analyses were coded by an LLM. The paper uses an LLM for the experiment, for the code that measures it, for the literature search and for the text, from the same vendor; a reviewer will ask for independence checks (a human verified the key numbers; the numbers trace to run folders, which is good, but the folders are private).
- The experiment-design decisions (keep rule, held-out redefinition, collapse detector) were changed after seeing results; a table of all protocol changes and their dates should be in the paper.

## 5. Recommended changes (the five that would most improve acceptance chances, in order)

1. Deliver part 6, the zoo comparison, as a descriptive survey (cheap; see section 6). It is the only part of the paper that speaks to the whole community, makes "a variety of models" concrete, and tests whether the three observations of 6.3 are general. Without it the paper is a case study of six models in one project. Present results as a small number of figures and one table, with independent lineages identified, and report which observations fail.
2. Add a positive control and fix the practice-set design: planted defects (cheap), and repeat at least one session per family with a practice set from a validation split disjoint from training and test. Replace the "no gain" narrative with a two-sided statement: where the method can find defects, where it cannot, and why.
3. Reframe the paper and the title around what it can support: an audit of weight reading and weight editing for ATR with an honest negative result, plus the data-quality findings. Retitle (e.g. "What can be read from, and edited in, the weights of text recognition models? A pre-specified negative result with kraken") and drop the claim of what an LLM "learned". State at the start that the programmer is a single-shot code generator, and run the controls that define whether the LLM contributes anything (blind, hand-written list, sham edits; cheap).
4. Repair the statistics and the over-reaching claims: cluster bootstrap by page/object, a multiplicity statement, a plot of predicted versus measured effects, replace the "interactions dominate" claim by a proper additivity test (global interpolation; cheap), measure the logit margins at deleted frames, remove "preregistered" or deposit the preregistrations externally, and correct the internal contradictions listed in 4.1 (seven vs six models, spec/parameter count, cross-references, 9.55 figures, 7 vs 8 hypotheses, "left passes unspent").
5. Make it reproducible and readable for the target readership: archive code, preregistrations, logs, sparse diffs, line lists and the model files (Zenodo, licence); release prompts; add a glossary box and a schematic of a recogniser; spell out BLB and the Bern data; cut the text by about a third by moving the forensics (6.4, 7.6) and the long session tables to an appendix; delete all `[TODO]` items.

| f | Margin analysis at deleted frames: blank minus best character logit at the frames where a space or `e` is dropped, before and after the blank-bias edits | Turns 8.2 from assertion into measurement | Cheap (hours) |
| g | Seed-variance null for readings: two fine-tunes of the same parent on the same data with different seeds (or a second fine-tune run with different shuffle), compare the weight-difference statistics with the real pairs | Baseline for "consistent traces of fine-tuning" | Moderate: needs two short training runs on a GPU (each a few hours at most for a 4 M model); the `hertwig-v1`/`v2` pair is a first approximation already in hand |
| h | C4: retrain `combined-v1` on the corrected data (the 22 October export) through the trainer; compare with the parent, the sibling and the best edit | The honest comparator for "undo label noise"; also gives the true cost of the noise | Moderate (one training job; the plan says it needs explicit approval) |
| i | The remaining protocol controls: uniform average of siblings, sham edits of the same size, blind programmer | The protocol itself says they are needed to interpret RQ2 | Cheap |
| j | An ablation on what the programmer sees: raw-tensor access in the sandbox (as in the blog) versus authors' tables versus tables with anonymised tensor names; log which statistics the hypotheses cite | The only route to saying anything about how an LLM "reads" weights (part 3 premise) and about the quality of its reading | Cheap to moderate |
| k | Adapter track: a small LoRA of a vision-language page-transcription model, with a known defect (repetition loops/truncation), same protocol | The only experiment that touches a genuine LLM's weights; the plan already describes it | Expensive (GPU, new tooling); optional for this paper, mandatory if part 9 is to be claimed |

## 7. How to handle parts 8 and 9: can the narrative still work?

**Part 8 (combination).** The draft is honest about what it found. To make it a result and not just a recount, tabulate every hypothesis (15) with the reading that motivated it, the predicted effect, the measured effect on practice and held-out sets, and a skill statement; add the ablation (j) and the planted-defect control (b). Be careful not to present the data-defect pathway as the product of weight reading: the text in 8.3 already says reading did not find the swap, which is right and should be kept prominent.

**Part 9 (what was learned about the inner functioning of LLMs).** I agree with the authors' own conclusion in 9.3: these experiments are on 4 M-parameter CNN-BiLSTM-CTC models; the LLM is only the programmer; nothing here measures how an LLM represents or processes text, and any sentence that suggests otherwise would be an overclaim. This is the honest position and I would keep it, but then part 9 should not appear in the narrative, title, or abstract as a result. There are three ways to keep a part 9 in the story:
1. Move it from "inner functioning of LLMs" to "what the experiment shows about an LLM acting as an analyst of weights": calibration of its predicted effects (it overestimated by a factor of 5-50, mostly directionally right), what information its hypotheses used, whether it cites the statistics it was given, whether blind or tables-only variants differ. This is behavioural evidence about LLM competence, legitimate for a DH venue interested in LLMs as research tools, but it needs controls (b, c, i, j) and more than two runs, and it must be called behaviour, not internals.
2. Turn part 9 into the argument for the transfer: state precisely which claims about LLMs (e.g. a refusal or repetition direction mediated by few weights) follow from the blog post's results and why kraken experiments cannot test them, and present the kraken study as a controlled small-network precursor that identifies the failure modes (practice set = training data, memorised donors, bias levers that trade deletions for insertions). That is a modest but real contribution and fits the title if rephrased.
3. Run the adapter track (k) and report it as the part-9 experiment, with the weights of an actual language model. Only this gives a statement about LLM internals, and even then about adapter deltas, not about the base model.
Options 1 and 2 are feasible before submission; option 3 is a separate paper. I recommend 1 + 2, with the title changed accordingly, and an explicit sentence in the abstract that the study makes no claim about LLM internals.
