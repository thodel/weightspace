## F1. Reinterpreting CTC training as iterative fitting
- Full text: arXiv 1904.10619v2 (7 Jul 2020), https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.10619 (preprint; header reads "Preprint submitted to Journal of LaTeX Templates", Pattern Recognition venue not printed in it, UNVERIFIED from source).
- Authors, year, venue: Hongzhu Li, Weiqiang Wang (Univ. of Chinese Academy of Sciences), 2020, Pattern Recognition (per brief; doi 10.1016/j.patcog.2020.107392).
- Model analysed: two things. (a) A "randomly initialized matrix" used as stand-in for network output, updated by the CTC gradient only (sec 2.4, Figs 1-6). (b) A real CRNN (Shi et al.) trained in Caffe on Synth90k, input 32x100, batch 100, best-path decoding, evaluated on IIIT5k/SVT/IC03/IC13 (sec 3.1-3.2); Synth5k for the generalisation test (sec 3.6).
- Question asked: why do CTC-trained outputs become spiky (label spikes separated by confident blanks), and can the spikiness be controlled and training sped up.
- Assessment method:
  - Rewrites the CTC gradient as a frame-wise cross-entropy against a "pseudo ground truth" y'_k^t = (1/p(l|Y)) * sum over paths pi in B^-1(l) with pi_t = k of p(pi|Y) (eq. 10). This is the CTC forward-backward posterior per frame.
  - Plots output Y against pseudo-GT Y' per iteration in a simulation (sec 2.4, Figs 2, 4). Identifies a "suppression phase" (blank dominates, label mass falls to zero) and a "peaking phase".
  - Intervention 1: rescale the pseudo-GT so the non-blank share is alpha (eqs 13-16; alpha = 0.5/0.7/0.9 shown in Fig 7). Intervention 2: reweight the gradient of "key frames" with gamma (sec 2.6).
  - Real-network test: Table 2 (SGD, lr 0.01/0.001/0.0001), Table 4 (AdaDelta), Table 5 (Synth5k, 150 and 300 epochs), Table 6 (comparison with CRNN and EnCTC). Control is plain CTC with the same settings. Single run per cell, no seeds/variance reported (mine).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: the explanation is derived analytically (the equivalence is by construction of the gradient) and illustrated by simulation; the paper then says "We conduct experiments on real-world data and get the results that are highly consistent with the simulation." (sec 3.4, Fig 7). Fig 7 shows the argmax output of one example image ("D- - - R- I I -F-T- -E- -D-" for plain CTC versus wide labels for alpha 0.9). No quantitative measure of spikiness (e.g. blank fraction, spike width) is reported (mine).
- Main finding: spikes arise because (1) the target distribution is always steeper than the current output, since smooth paths put mass on wrong label sequences, and (2) blank is the dominant class early, so non-blank mass first collapses ("suppression phase"). Fixing the non-blank proportion alpha widens label activations "on the time axis" and speeds convergence.
- Limits (stated): "we have not found an 'optimal hyperparameter' that is effective in all situations" (sec 3.7); alpha/gamma depend on training-set scale. Rescaling is "highly simplified": equal share for all labels, per batch not per sequence (sec 2.5). Authors also say the near-converged benefit "may bring risks or benefits" (sec 3.7).
  Limits (mine): analysis of the spiky phenomenon is on a directly-optimised output matrix, not on a network where weights are shared across frames; only word images (Synth90k), not lines; the effect of alpha on accuracy is mixed (AdaDelta Table 4 shows no consistent gain); no look inside a trained net's hidden states.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it gives a concrete, checkable signature of what training does to the output layer (a blank-dominated posterior with 1-2 frame spikes, and a controllable non-blank share), so a weight edit can be assessed by whether it moves the blank/non-blank frame ratio and spike width rather than only CER.

## F2. Blank Collapse: Compressing CTC Emission for the Faster Decoding
- Full text: arXiv 2210.17017v2 (27 Jun 2023), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.17017 (text does not print "Interspeech 2023" except in reference list, UNVERIFIED from source; venue per brief).
- Authors, year, venue: Minkyu Jung (Channel Corporation), Ohhyeok Kwon, Seunghyun Seo, Sunshin Seo (Naver Cloud), 2023, Interspeech 2023 (per brief).
- Model analysed: wav2vec 2.0 BASE/LARGE character-level CTC models fine-tuned on 10 min / 100 h / 960 h of LibriSpeech, used without further training; decoding with torchaudio CTC beam search + 4-gram word LM, also Transformer LM and a WFST decoder (sec 4.1, 4.3).
- Question asked: how are blank frames distributed in CTC emissions, and can runs of blank frames be dropped before beam search without changing the transcript.
- Assessment method:
  - Defines a "strong blank frame" as one with blank probability > theta (sec 2.3, Def. 2) and drops blank frames at the start, end, and any blank frame that follows another blank (Def. 3, "consecutive extension"). Weak variant uses argmax = blank.
  - Counts frame statistics: run lengths of blank versus non-blank frames on LibriSpeech test-other (Fig 1); fraction of collapsible frames for theta = 0.999, 0.99, 0.9 and weak (Table 1, LARGE/960h).
  - Compares WER and real-time factor with and without collapse (Table 2, gamma (beam threshold) = 50, 1,500 beams, LM weight 1.57, length penalty -0.64, 32 batch); sweeps theta and gamma (Fig 3); varies model size and fine-tuning set (Table 3).
  - Includes a mathematical argument (eq. 2 beam update; blank-frame bound P_nonblank < 1 - theta).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: the collapse is checked against decoding output (WER unchanged to the third decimal at theta 0.99: test-clean 2.031 original vs 2.029, Table 2). No test of whether the collapsed frames carry information beyond WER (e.g. alignment, timestamps) except a remark that "timestep alignment" can be remapped (sec 3.3). Single runs, no variance (mine).
- Main finding: about 43-46% of CTC frames are collapsible (Table 1); Fig 1 caption: "Most of the non-blank frames last only one or two frames whereas blank frames seem to last longer." Better models are more confident: "a good model can tell whether a certain frame can be collapsed out or not." (sec 4.2). Decoding time falls by up to about 44% with negligible WER change.
- Limits (stated): sec 3.2: "this method always as a nonzero opportunity that could harm the original result" [sic]; collapsing non-blank runs is judged riskier and not attempted. With a Transformer LM "there is only 1% gain in decoding time when theta is 0.999" (sec 4.3). Theta = 0.9 gave less speed-up than 0.99, interpreted as over-collapsing dropping good candidates (sec 4.4).
  Limits (mine): speech only (wav2vec2 at 20 ms frames, character CTC, 4x-ish blank dominance); no analysis of what the blank frames encode internally; says nothing about CNN+BiLSTM line models whose frame rate and blank fraction differ.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it supplies a cheap, training-free probe (fraction of frames with blank probability above 0.99, and run lengths of non-blank argmax) that can be computed on any kraken output matrix before and after a weight edit to check that the blank/spike structure is intact.

## F3. Gradient-Based Speech-to-Text Alignment for Any ASR Model: From CTC to Speech LLMs
- Full text: arXiv 2607.06831v1 (7 Jul 2026), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06831
- Authors, year, venue: Albert Zeyer, Ralf Schlüter, Hermann Ney (RWTH Aachen / AppTek), 2026, arXiv preprint (cs.CL); no other venue printed.
- Model analysed: 16 models in four families: CTC (MMS-FA wav2vec2, XLS-R phoneme, Parakeet CTC, OWSM-CTC, streaming FastConformer-CTC), transducers, AED (Whisper, CrisperWhisper, OWLS), speech LLMs (Voxtral, Phi-4-MM, Canary-Qwen); MFA GM-HMM as reference (sec III).
- Question asked: can the gradient of a teacher-forced token log-probability w.r.t. the input give word boundaries for any model, and how does it compare with each model's native (posterior/attention) alignment.
- Assessment method:
  - For each transcript token s and frame t, takes G_{s,t} = log ||grad_{x_t} log p(a_s | a_<s, x)|| (L2 norm after testing p-norms; sec II). For CTC and transducers the token score is a prefix score computed by dynamic programming.
  - Applies log-softmax over time (optionally weighted by audio energy, rho = 0.5) and decodes by Viterbi over an automaton with blanks only at word boundaries (default) or CTC-like full blanks; silence score beta_t constant, z-score, or energy-based (eqs 2-4, Fig 2).
  - Scores against gold word boundaries on TIMIT-test and a 5 h Buckeye subset: word-boundary error (WBE) and share within a 50 ms collar (Table I); signed start/end offsets and width error (Table III); alignment of the model's own hypothesis with identity-gated F1 (Table II).
  - Ablations: norm type, grad vs grad x input, SmoothGrad/VarGrad/Integrated/Expected Gradients (numbers omitted), blank scheme, energy weight, tokenisation (char vs subword), depth at which the gradient is taken (Tables IX-XI), inter-CTC layers, time-stretch, compute cost.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: ground-truth boundaries (TIMIT/Buckeye gold) and a GM-HMM reference. Attention heads for the baselines were chosen on a labelled TIMIT dev set (sec III). That is the only validation; no perturbation/deletion test of faithfulness.
- Main finding: for CTC, posterior (native) alignment is more accurate than gradient for well-behaved models (MMS-FA Buckeye WBE 46 ms posteriors vs 121 ms gradients, Table I), but the gradient wins where the encoder is time-shifted: streaming FastConformer-CTC TIMIT WBE 357 ms posteriors vs 127 ms gradients (Table I). Table III shows CTC posteriors are shifted and narrow words (MMS-FA width error -60 ms, start +47 ms) whereas the gradient has width error +2 ms but a -93 ms start offset. Text: "The streaming posteriors' large error is largely a systematic emission-delay bias rather than scatter" (sec IV a). Gradient "is active even in silence and needs the energy-aware (lambda ~ 2) or z-score blank" (sec IV c), so it does not give blank behaviour for free. Best gradient is at intermediate encoder depth, not at the input (sec IV d). Character targets for non-autoregressive CTC "collapse" (worse than subwords, "in the worst cases by an order of magnitude", sec IV b).
- Limits (stated): "This work is not a proposal of gradient alignment as the best aligner" (sec I); cost is one backward pass per token; usually "somewhat behind a strong native aligner".
  Limits (mine): speech models with 20-80 ms frames, no handwriting; the alignment is validated against word boundaries only, so it shows where input energy influences a token, not that the model uses that evidence causally; the CTC variant needs prefix scores whose backward pass dominates cost.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it shows that CTC emission timing can be offset from the true location by a systematic delay, so if a weight edit is judged by where the spikes fall, spike position must be compared against input-gradient attribution (computed through the CTC prefix score) and not only against the argmax frame.

## F4. Explainability of Speech Recognition Transformers via Gradient-Based Attention Visualization
- Full text: abstract only (OpenAlex lists the paper as closed access, no open copy; no arXiv version found by title search; IEEE page not opened). Abstract read via OpenAlex record for doi 10.1109/tmm.2023.3282488.
- Authors, year, venue: Tianli Sun, Haonan Chen, Guosheng Hu, Lianghua He, Cairong Zhao (OpenAlex author list); IEEE Transactions on Multimedia, 2023 per OpenAlex (Crossref gives issue date 2024).
- Model analysed (abstract): ASR Transformers; experiments use CTC loss (point 1 below). Dataset and exact models UNVERIFIED (not in abstract).
- Question asked (abstract): which audio frames produce which output text, and can the explanation be used to improve the ASR model.
- Assessment method (abstract only): a gradient-based attention visualisation that "unravels the attention calculation from information flow of Transformer attention modules". Quote: "we propose a novel attention visualization method in ASR Transformers and try to explain which frames of the audio result in the output text." Three uses: (1) visualise attention with respect to CTC loss and train with "adversarial attention erasing regularization", reported to lower WER; (2) attention on specific words to show semantic/grammatical relations; (3) contrastive explanation for homophones. Hyperparameters, controls, metrics UNVERIFIED.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: none stated in the abstract (the abstract claims "more efficient and intuitively understandable" than other attention-visualisation methods; no faithfulness test mentioned). Rest UNVERIFIED.
- Main finding (abstract): the visualisation can be used to regularise training (erasing attended regions lowers WER) and to show word relations and homophone handling. Whether the attribution goes through the CTC output is only implied by "attention with respect to CTC loss" (UNVERIFIED).
- Limits: stated: none in abstract. (mine) attention-based, so it applies to Transformer encoders, not to conv + BiLSTM stacks; "attention erasing improves WER" is a training-utility result, not an independent check that the maps are faithful.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: little direct use (attention-specific); at most it is a second example, after F3, of attribution taken with respect to the CTC loss.

## F5. Version 5 of the Kraken ATR Engine for the Humanities
- Full text: https://hal.science/hal-05144723/document (HAL v2, CC BY 4.0, 18 pages, 13 Jul 2025).
- Authors, year, venue: Benjamin Kiessling (ALMAnaCH Inria; EPHE-PSL; UMR 8546 CNRS), ICDAR 2025 (19th Int. Conf. on Document Analysis and Recognition, Sep 2025, Wuhan).
- Model analysed: kraken 5 default recogniser (system description, not an analysis of what it learns).
- Question asked: design principles of kraken and what version 5 adds (trainable reading order, unsupervised recognition pretraining, new model repository).
- Assessment method: none for network behaviour. Evaluation is only CER on public models "as reported by model authors", on IAM, and on training-size/pretraining comparisons (Table 2: CER on 50/100/200 lines from scratch vs unsupervised pretraining, e.g. 60.85 vs 58.94 at 50 lines, one French manuscript, 300-line test set, no variance) plus a fine-tuning curve from CATMuS 1.5 (Fig 4, graph by Thibault Clérice from Naples Dante Project data).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: none stated (no model-analysis section; no blank/alignment/attribution analysis; the word "blank" does not occur in the paper).
- Main finding / facts relevant to weight-space editing:
  - Architecture (sec 3.2, Table 1): "a hybrid CNN-LSTM ... made up of an stack of convolutional layers designed to extract local visual features followed by three LSTM layers". Table 1 default: conv 3x13x32, 2D dropout 0.1, maxpool 2x2; conv 3x13x32, dropout, maxpool; conv 3x9x64, dropout, maxpool; conv 3x9x64, dropout; 3 bidirectional LSTMs, hidden size 200, with dropout after each (printed as "Dropout 2D, p = 0.1" at layers 12 and 14 and "1D, p = 0.5" at layer 16; reproduced as printed); final FC layer "variable size" (output alphabet).
  - CTC: "line-wise recognizer based on a neural network trained with CTC loss which predicts texts one line at a time". Architecture is set with VGSL ("Variable Graph Specification Language"), so layer sizes vary by model.
  - Labels and codec (sec 3): "the recognizer predicts for an input line image a sequence of labels which are then mapped one by one into Unicode code points"; outputs are in display order and reshuffled with the BiDi algorithm; "Kraken allows arbitrary codecs mapping between labels and Unicode code point space in a many-to-many fashion" (decomposition, aggregation, private-use codes). So the output layer index is not tied to code points.
  - Language models: "training data inefficient methods such as postcorrection with language modelling or transformer-based text recognizers are avoided" (sec 3). No LM in the pipeline.
  - Preprocessing: line images scaled to fixed height; local dewarping by piecewise affine transform on baseline + polygon (Fig 3).
  - Pretraining (sec 3.2): self-supervised, reimplementation of [39] with reconstruction loss on masked parts of unlabelled lines "to learn visual representations in the convolutional and recurrent encoder layers"; then fine-tuned with CTC by "adding a randomly initialized linear projection layer".
  - Model formats/serialisation: only the data-interchange side is described (PageXML, ALTO, hOCR, abbyyXML, Jinja templates; sec 3.1). The weight file format (e.g. safetensors/coreml) is NOT stated; segmentation model files carry configuration "metadata". The repository follows HTRMoPo with Zenodo DOIs and a model-card recommendation; 48 models, 18 writing systems (sec 3.3).
- Limits (stated): conclusion/outlook notes layout analysis errors are "a direct cause of misrecognition". (mine) no analysis of the recogniser's behaviour, no ablation, Table 2 CER values for public models are author-reported and not comparable across datasets.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it gives the exact default layer stack (3 conv + 3 BiLSTM of 200 + FC, VGSL-defined) and the codec indirection between output index and code point, so an edit to the output layer or LSTM weights must respect model-specific VGSL shapes and codec mappings, and no paper-side analysis tool exists to lean on.

## F6. Improving Automatic Text Recognition with Language Models in the PyLaia Open-Source Library
- Full text: arXiv 2404.18722v1 (29 Apr 2024), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.18722 (preprint does not print the ICDAR 2024 venue, per brief).
- Authors, year, venue: Solène Tarride, Yoann Schneider, Marie Generali-Lince, Mélodie Boillet, Bastien Abadie, Christopher Kermorvant (TEKLIA, Paris), 2024, ICDAR 2024 (per brief; doi 10.1007/978-3-031-70549-6_23).
- Model analysed: PyLaia CRNN trained with CTC (sec 5.1): four conv blocks (kernel 3, pooling 2 except block 3, channels 12/24/48/48, batch norm, LeakyReLU) then three bidirectional LSTM blocks of 256 units, dropout 0.5, line height 128; same architecture for all 12 datasets.
- Question asked: how much do (a) calibrated confidence scores and (b) character/subword/word n-gram LMs combined at CTC beam-search time improve PyLaia.
- Assessment method:
  - Confidence: five per-line scores computed from the softmax (mean max-softmax before CTC collapse, mean after collapse "excluded from the calculation" of blank and duplicate frames, top1-top2 difference, entropy, Monte Carlo dropout). Each is correlated (Spearman, Pearson) with 1-CER on NorHand v1 (Table 2). Temperature scaling T chosen by grid search 1 to 6 (step 0.5) on validation, effect on correlation on test sets (Table 3).
  - LM: 6-gram character KenLM/SRILM-style model with modified Kneser-Ney, built per dataset from its own training transcriptions, LM weight 1.5 "optimal values measured on the NorHand v3 dataset" (sec 5.1); decoding with torchaudio `ctc_decoder` beam search. CER/WER with and without LM on 12 datasets (Table 4). Comparison with state of the art (Table 5). Computation time in appendix 7.1.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: for confidence, correlation with recognition rate (best: mean softmax after CTC, Spearman 0.60 / Pearson 0.62 on NorHand v1; entropy 0.32/0.34). For LM, before/after error rates only; one hyperparameter setting tuned on a single dataset and reused. No per-dataset tuning, no variance.
- Main finding: character LM lowers average CER by 11.9% (0.9 absolute points) and WER by 12.9% (3.3 points); benefit largest on CASIA (logographic); negative or small where CER is under 2% (NewsEye, Esposalles: "the language model tends to create more errors than it can correct", sec 6.2). Confidence is poorly calibrated: "most confidence scores falling in the [0.97-0.99] range" (sec 4.1), so temperature scaling is needed. The authors also remark that low results on private tables "suggest that the CTC mechanism may not be well suited for such documents featuring blank areas and abbreviations" (sec 6.3), without testing it.
- Limits (stated): "There are two major limitations of PyLaia. First, its reliance on CTC, which limits training to line-level recognition" and it is not transformer based (sec 6.3). Monte Carlo dropout excluded because it degraded recognition.
  Limits (mine): the LM acts only outside the network; nothing shows what the optical model itself already encodes of language (no run with a shuffled or foreign-language LM, no per-character LM contribution); the LM weight is tuned on one dataset; the "blank areas" explanation is conjecture.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it fixes the baseline protocol (best-path versus beam search with a character n-gram, CER/WER on 12 datasets) and shows that LM gains vanish when the optical model is already very good, so any claim that a weight edit "adds language knowledge" must be compared against a decoder-side n-gram and not against raw best-path output.

## F7a. N-gram Injection into Transformers for Dynamic Language Model Adaptation in Handwritten Text Recognition
- Full text: arXiv 2603.03930v2 (10 Mar 2026), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.03930 (preprint does not print ICDAR 2026, per brief; the Kaggle split link carries "papericdar2026").
- Authors, year, venue: Florent Meyer, Laurent Guichard (ANTAI, Rennes), Yann Soullard, Denis Coquenet, Guillaume Gravier, Bertrand Coüasnon (Univ Rennes, CNRS, IRISA), 2026, ICDAR 2026 (per brief).
- Model analysed: autoregressive FCN-transformers, not CTC: TrOCR-small (char tokenizer), DAN, SaLT, and the authors' 2.1M-parameter "word attention network" (WAN: ten-layer FCN encoder, two-layer transformer decoder) with the n-gram injected into the decoder input (NGI). CTC/BiLSTM networks only appear in related work.
- Question asked: do transformer HTR decoders absorb the source-corpus language and fail when the test language is shifted, and can an external character n-gram be swapped in at test time instead.
- Assessment method:
  - Builds language-shifted splits of IAM and RIMES words ("lexicon split": disjoint lexicons; "k-means split": words clustered on bigram indicator vectors, k = 2) plus a private name-to-surname form set N2S (Table 1 reports 5-gram character perplexity source vs target).
  - Trains on source only, 100 epochs, cross-entropy; reports CER on source and target test (Tables 2-5). NGI uses a source n-gram on source test and a target n-gram (separate text set NG, not the test set) on target test. Post-hoc lattice rescoring with SRILM (beam 150) as comparison (+LM).
  - Ablation (Table 6, IAM/RIMES k-means): remove noise on the first n-gram vector, remove teacher-forcing error TFE, use 3-gram/2-gram.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: the claim that the decoder holds a source language model is supported behaviourally: source CER stays about equal while target CER doubles from lexicon to k-means splits, in step with perplexity (sec 6.3); e.g. WAN IAM k-means 7.6 to 23.4 CER, RIMES 4.3 to 29.9 (Table 2, WAN trained without TFE, footnote 3). The statement "we hypothesize that character features learnt by a vision-encoder are robust enough" (sec 3) is a hypothesis, not tested (no encoder probing or encoder swap). Single runs, no variance reported (mine).
- Main finding: NGI lowers target CER with source CER kept: IAM k-means target 18.0 to 10.1, RIMES k-means 25.4 to 19.2 (Table 4); N2S target CER 5.0 (WAN) to 4.5 (WAN+NGI), source 2.2 to 2.4 (Table 5, as extracted; Table 3 gives 6.3 for the WAN trained without TFE). Post-processing +LM is slightly better than NGI but costlier; both combined are best. Lower n-gram order reduces target gains (Table 6).
- Limits (stated): the N2S gain is "minimal", attributed to a census database with erroneous fields and target PPL 18.9 (sec 6.4). Evaluated at word level only; "could be applied to any architecture relying on an auto-regressive character-level transformer decoder".
  Limits (mine): in-text numbers for the baseline WAN differ between tables (Table 2 trained without TFE); no evidence about CTC models; the LM injection and the test labels share the same alphabet/lexicon construction, so the shift is synthetic by design.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it supplies a tested protocol (source/target language shift with perplexity-matched splits, CER before and after) for asking whether a model's language prior can be changed without touching the visual encoder, which is the question a weight edit of the BiLSTM stack would pose; but the paper is on attention decoders, so CTC BiLSTMs remain unmeasured.

## F7b. Learning to Align: Addressing Character Frequency Distribution Shifts in Handwritten Text Recognition
- Full text: ACL Anthology https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.1014.pdf (also arXiv 2506.09846v2, 20 Sep 2025).
- Authors, year, venue: Panagiotis Kaliosis (Stony Brook; Archimedes/Athena RC), John Pavlopoulos (Athena RC; AUEB), 2025, Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2025, pp. 18667-18684 (printed in the Anthology PDF).
- Model analysed: C-RNN (Shi et al.; "CNN ... RNN ... trained using the CTC loss", trained from scratch; details of layers not given in the text I read), TrOCR (cross-entropy, fine-tuned), and Whisper for ASR. Data: HPGT (1,698 Greek lines, 10th-16th c.), CATMuS-FR (3,401 lines, 13th-16th c.), a synthetic Greek set from four LLM styles, EdAcc for ASR.
- Question asked: can the character-frequency distribution of the predicted text be pulled toward an empirical per-century/region distribution (training loss and/or decoding score) to handle intra-dataset shifts.
- Assessment method:
  - Frequency analysis per century (Fig 1, Appendix A/B correlations).
  - Adds a Wasserstein-2 loss between the predicted character-frequency vector (computed "from the model's output logits") and the sample's century/region target; total loss lambda*CE + (1-lambda)*W2 (Algorithm 1; for C-RNN the CE slot is CTC, text says "CTC or Cross-Entropy"). Guided decoding adds a W2 penalty to beam scores (eq. 2).
  - Baselines: plain fine-tuning/training, and "TR-TT", a prepended temporal/regional tag token. Metrics CER/WER, 3 seeds, mean and SEM (Tables 1, 2); ablation training-only vs decoding-only (Table 4); beam size 1/5/7; per-century breakdown (Appendix F); lambda grid 0.05 to 0.95 on validation (Appendix G; 0.3-0.6 "generally" best); lipogram generation test.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: gains are reported only as CER/WER differences, e.g. HPGT C-RNN 24.72 to 23.89 CER (SEM 0.38/0.34), CATMuS C-RNN 17.08 to 16.79 (SEM 0.29/0.52) (Table 1): several of these are inside one SEM (mine). The paper does not describe how a differentiable character-frequency vector is obtained from CTC frame outputs (blank handling, collapse) in the text I read: UNVERIFIED/not stated. No check that the output distribution actually moved toward the target.
- Main finding about context: tagging the input with century/region ("TR-TT") did not help C-RNN and hurt on CATMuS (WER 67.61 vs 54.79 baseline, Table 1, as extracted), whereas the frequency loss helped slightly; authors argue tagging controls the model "in an implicit way that is hard to interpret or adjust" (sec 5).
- Limits (stated): needs empirical frequency distributions per domain; unigram only; efficiency (Limitations section).
  Limits (mine): small effects on the CTC model; no look at what C-RNN's blank/spike structure does under the loss; TR-TT for a CNN-RNN is not described (how a tag token enters), so the baseline may be weak.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it shows that a character-frequency prior can be imposed on the output of a CTC CNN-RNN only via loss/decoding, and that a naive context tag can fail on such a model, so a weight edit that claims to shift character priors should be checked against both a unigram-prior decoding baseline and a tag baseline.

## F8. Does Context Matter? Enhancing Handwritten Text Recognition with Metadata in Historical Manuscripts
- Full text: https://hal.science/hal-04704547/document (HAL v2, CC BY 4.0, 16 Oct 2024; CEUR copy not fetched).
- Authors, year, venue: Benjamin Kiessling (EPHE-PSL), Thibault Clérice (Inria), CHR 2024 Computational Humanities Research Conference, Dec 2024, Aarhus.
- Model analysed: size-reduced Conformer encoder (dim 144, 16 layers, 4 heads, depthwise-conv subsampling factor 4) plus a single fully connected decoder, trained with CTC, on CATMuS Medieval (train/val from release 1.0.1, test from 1.5.0); compared with kraken's default CNN+LSTM recogniser ("Kraken"). Line height 96 px, AdamW, 100 epochs, same hyperparameters and seeds (sec 5.1).
- Question asked: does supplying line metadata (century, script type, language) improve recognition, and does a model that must predict the metadata (auxiliary loss) do the same.
- Assessment method:
  - Semantic context token: multi-hot metadata vector expanded to width w and concatenated to the input image columns (sec 3.2, Fig 1), CTC as usual. Variants: each field alone or all three.
  - Auxiliary loss: side branch after the encoder (adaptive max-pool + FC) predicts the token with BCE; L = (1-w)*L_CTC + w*L_aux, w in {0.1, 0.2, 0.3} (sec 3.3).
  - Metrics: character accuracy micro and macro (per-document mean) (Table 4); per-manuscript dispersion (Fig 3, Table 5, 6); ablation where the token is zeroed at test time (Table 7).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: the "zeroed token" ablation is the only test of whether the model uses the metadata. Results (Table 7, micro/macro): All 91.14/92.86 vs All (zeroed) 91.13/92.79, i.e. no change; single-field models drop when zeroed (Century 87.53 to 85.52, Language 88.37 to 86.55, Script 87.73 to 87.22 micro). Zeroing is a distribution-shift control only; swapping in a wrong token was not tested (mine).
- Main finding: Conformer baseline 90.32 micro vs kraken CNN+LSTM 86.56 (Table 4); Conformer + all three tokens 91.14 micro, 92.86 macro; single-token models are worse than baseline (87.5-88.4); auxiliary-loss models reach 89.6-90.0 and "half did not converge" (12 configurations, sec 6), with instability "unrelated to the chosen weight w". Authors read the All model's tiny drop when zeroed as having "effectively learned to separate features, even without manually provided context" (sec 6, ablation). Quote: "The minimal variation in accuracy between the zeroed-out context and the full context (< 0.15 percentage points)".
- Limits (stated): only works if the same metadata are known at inference; multi-hot tokens, no embedding; with a "very lightweight FFN decoder" metadata was put at the input and a better-separated visual/linguistic architecture is future work (sec 7). Authors also say some tokens (language, genre) "are of more importance to the latter [linguistic model] than the former".
  Limits (mine): single run per configuration (identical seed) so differences of 0.3-0.8 points cannot be separated from seed noise; the near-zero zeroing effect for the All model also fits the reading that the gain comes from training with the token (regularisation/representation) and not from using it at inference, which the paper does not distinguish; no probe of encoder features for metadata; comparison to kraken CNN+LSTM mixes architecture and metadata effects; the model is a Conformer, not a BiLSTM stack.
- Relevance to weight-space edits of a CTC line recogniser: it is the closest published test of "context" in a kraken-adjacent CTC model, and its zeroing control (accuracy unchanged when the context input is removed) is the template to check whether a weight edit that is meant to encode context actually changes outputs.
