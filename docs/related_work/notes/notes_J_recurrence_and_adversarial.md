## J1. Coquenet et al., "Have convolutions already made recurrence obsolete ...?"
- Full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.04954 (arXiv abs 2012.04954v1, posted 9 Dec 2020; 6 pp). A HAL copy was searched for once (HAL search page) and not found; arXiv used.
- Authors, year, venue: Denis Coquenet, Yann Soullard, Clement Chatelain, Thierry Paquet (LITIS, Univ. Rouen). Note: Soullard is also an author (the brief lists three). arXiv journal_ref: "2019 International Conference on Document Analysis and Recognition Workshops (ICDARW)". The arXiv posting is 2020; the venue year is 2019 as the journal_ref gives it.
- Model analysed: CNN+BLSTM baseline "as in [26]" (Sec. III-A): 8 conv layers (32 to 256 units), 4 max-pool, dropout, dense, 2 BLSTM(256), dense n+1, softmax, CTC (Fig. 1a). Input resized to 32 px height; 32x32 px sliding window, stride 4 (Sec. III). No language model, no lexicon. Compared with a recurrence-free gated CNN ("G-CNN", up to 512 units, gated blocks, residuals, depthwise-separable and shared-weight convs, Fig. 1b) and a "CNN+Dense only" variant.
- Question asked: "whether recurrence is still necessary for modeling dependencies within signals such as handwriting" (Sec. I); and the contribution of the BLSTM layers (Sec. III-B, IV-D1).
- Method: (a) Sec. IV-D1, Table I: BLSTM layers removed from the baseline, leaving CNN + dense; trained and scored as a separate model (training time 1h10 is reported, so it was retrained, not a post-hoc cut: mine, inferred). (b) Alternative: a purpose-built recurrence-free G-CNN, with its own ablation (Table II: depthwise-separable convs, max-pool placement, shared weights, gate depth, gate removal; these ablate G-CNN components, not recurrence). (c) Table III: RIMES vs RIMES+lined-paper background; Table IV: data augmentation (7x). Dataset: RIMES lines, 9,947 train / 1,333 val / 778 test, 100 characters (French); plus a synthetic grid-background variant. Metric: CER, parameters, training time (time for CER to fall below 105% of its minimum). Optimiser: Adam, lr 1e-4, momentum 0.9 (Sec. III-B/Training details). Only RIMES; IAM and other datasets not used.
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: none stated. No repeated runs, seeds, variances or significance tests stated (searched text for seed/average/std: nothing). No analysis of what the BLSTM computes (no per-frame, window-size or context-length probe).
- Main finding: Table I numbers, test CER: CNN+BLSTM 6.88 (4.1 M params), CNN+Dense only 19.03 (1.5 M), G-CNN 10.03 (6.9 M). Verbatim: "BLSTM layers represent more than half of the total number of trainable parameters" and they "lower the CER from 19.03% down to 6.88%" (Sec. IV-D1). Background: both lose about 2.5 points CER (BLSTM 6.88 to 9.27, G-CNN 10.03 to 12.55; Table III). Conclusion (Sec. V): "standard models based on CNN and BLSTM layers seem still current"; G-CNN trains faster (10h vs 1d22h59) but is harder to tune. On context specifically: only an indirect, unsupported remark in Sec. IV-D4: "CNN+BLSTM which compensates with its better ability to use the context".
- Limits (stated): G-CNN "not easy to use", no "self-sufficient stackable block" as with LSTM (Sec. V). (mine): The ablation removes the layer and retrains a different network, so it measures architecture plus optimisation, not what a trained recurrent layer does. The dense-only model's context is whatever the 32 px window and convs give; window size was fixed, so "recurrence vs context" is confounded with window width (hedged: the paper does not say how dense is applied over time). Single dataset, single run per cell, no CIs. Gap of 12 CER points shows recurrence matters for this setup, not how (which context range, which characters).
- Relevance: Shows that the 3-BiLSTM stack in a kraken line model carries most of the parameters and most of the accuracy only in a train-from-scratch comparison, so it gives no evidence on which weights encode context, which is the gap a weight-space edit/ablation of a trained model would fill.

## J2. Xu et al., "What Machines See Is Not What They Get: Fooling Scene Text Recognition Models With Adversarial Text Images"
- Full text: https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Xu_What_Machines_See_Is_Not_What_They_Get_Fooling_Scene_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf (11 pp; footer page numbers start 12304; CVF html page: content_CVPR_2020/html/Xu_..._CVPR_2020_paper.html). Note: the CVF filename is "..._Fooling_Scene_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf" (truncated title).
- Authors, year, venue: Xing Xu, Jiefu Chen, Jinhui Xiao, Lianli Gao, Fumin Shen, Heng Tao Shen (UESTC). CVPR 2020 (CVF open-access version).
- Model analysed: five STR models from the Baek et al. [1] framework: three CTC-based (CRNN, Rosetta, STAR-Net) and two attention-based (RARE, TRBA); pretrained on MJ+ST synthetic data, weights as released by [1]; recognition of single cropped words, not lines. Paper says VGG/ResNet feature extractors and "Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM) is used as the (de)-selection in sequence modeling" (Sec. 4.1). (mine, UNVERIFIED in this paper: from memory of Baek et al., Rosetta has no BiLSTM, so the blanket statement may not hold for all five.)
- Question asked: Can STR models, CTC and attention, be fooled by small perturbations; first attack on sequence-prediction STR (Sec. 1). Not a study of how the models use context.
- Method: white-box optimisation of an additive perturbation delta (Eq. 1): min L(x',l') + lambda*||delta||_2^2, x' in [-1,1]. For CTC (Sec. 3.2) L is the negative log-sum of probabilities over valid alignments of the target (Eq. 2-3); for a targeted attack with k changed characters it keeps only the prefix up to the last changed character (Eq. 5-6, "max p(pi'|x') proportional to max(y...)"), treating the unchanged suffix probability as a fixed factor. Attention models: analogous loss on the autoregressive probabilities (Sec. 3.3). Algorithm 1: normalised-gradient steps, learning rate annealed 0.1 to 0.01, clip to norm 0.2, early stop; lambda binary-searched in [1e-3, 1e4]; PyTorch, GTX 1080 Ti. Targeted case tested: target 1 or 2 edits (substitution mainly; Table 4 insertion/substitution/deletion on ICDAR15) from ground truth; also untargeted. Data: 7 real sets (CUTE80, IC03, IC13, IC15, IIIT5K, SVT, SP) plus 2 synthetic test sets of 4000 images each (MJ, ST). Metrics: success rate SR, mean L2 distance, mean iterations. Further: cross-model transfer (Table 3), perturbation visualisation across iterations (Fig. 2), effect of mu and lambda (Fig. 3), and 800 adversarial images submitted to the Baidu OCR API (Fig. 4).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: for attack success, SR on the model's own output is direct. For what the models do with the input: none stated. No ablation of RNN/context, no per-frame/saliency analysis; the only qualitative evidence is perturbation images (Fig. 2). Cross-model transfer is the only behavioural comparison across architectures.
- Main finding: "all the STR models are vulnerable to adversarial examples, as the SRs on them reach almost 100%" (Sec. 4.2); targeted attacks need about 20-50 iterations, untargeted under 10, mean L2 about 1.0 or less. Cross-model: asymmetric; "The CRNN model obtains the best average SR score while ... TRBA ... the worst" (Sec. 4.2, Table 3), i.e. adversarial examples crafted on CRNN fool other models best. Baidu OCR: "SR is more than 20%" (Sec. 4.3; Fig. 4 table values 20.90 to 26.70, mapping to model/mode UNVERIFIED). Fig. 1 caption: "we can mislead the models to predict 'cvpr', 'ieee' and '2020'".
- Limits (stated): the commercial-system drop is attributed partly to a possibly different character vocabulary (Sec. 4.3). (mine): Intro asserts the RNN encoder is what makes STR "more difficult" ("leverages RNN structures instead of CNNs to capture the sequential context", Sec. 1) but never tests it. Full white-box access required. Single words only, no controls such as random noise of equal L2 norm are reported (none found in the text). Algorithm 1 as printed ends "until R(x') = l", which reads as a typo for the target/"not l" (mine).
- Relevance: It confirms that a CTC recogniser's output can be steered by a perturbation of L2 about 1 using only gradients through the CTC loss, but it says nothing about which layers carry context, so it is a behavioural probe of input sensitivity, not a probe of weights.

## J3. Song and Shmatikov, "Fooling OCR Systems with Adversarial Text Images"
- Full text: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.05385 (arXiv:1802.05385v1 [cs.LG], 15 Feb 2018, 15 pp; only v1 seen).
- Authors, year, venue: Congzheng Song, Vitaly Shmatikov (Cornell University / Cornell Tech). Year 2018. Venue: arXiv preprint; no venue stated in the PDF.
- Model analysed: Tesseract 4.00 alpha line recogniser (open model "tessdata_best"). This is the same family as kraken: VGSL-specified conv + recurrent + CTC. Table 1 / Sec. 2: input grey line image h x w; conv2d 3x3x16 tanh; maxpool 3x3; 4 LSTM layers with 64, 96, 96, 512 hidden units (modes f/r, x/y); output layer 111 units, w/3 probability vectors; beam-search decoding. Weights loaded into the authors' TensorFlow re-implementation (copied from tensorflow/models research/street).
- Question asked: Can an adversary craft slightly perturbed images of text so a deep-learning OCR outputs adversary-chosen text (e.g. antonyms), and what does this do to downstream NLP?
- Method: white-box (Sec. 3.1: "complete access to the entire OCR pipeline"). Targeted attack by min c*L_CTC(f(x'),t') + ||x-x'||_2^2 with box constraint via tanh change of variables (Sec. 3.2), Adam. Single words: 120 WordNet antonym pairs within an edit-distance threshold (2, 3 or 4 for words of <=5, 6-9, >9 chars), 16 fonts, 1,000 iterations, step 0.01, c=20 (Sec. 4.2). Whole documents: Hillary Clinton emails; 20 Newsgroups categorisation and Rotten Tomatoes sentiment (2,000 iterations, c=30 or 200 as stated), 300/250 adversarial texts. Control: adversarial images were evaluated "by directly applying Tesseract", not only by the TF port (Sec. 4.1), which checks port fidelity. Rejection rate vs L2 on 500 word images (Fig. 9); one printed-and-scanned example (Fig. 8).
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: success is measured on the real Tesseract output; for single words "over 90% of the words" succeed for most fonts (Sec. 4.2, Table 2; per-font numbers not extracted). Statements about internal behaviour have no experiment: the claim that "internal feature representations depend on the context" (Sec. 3.2) is argued from the architecture, not measured.
- Main finding: perturbations are small (they say they affect "only a tiny fraction of the pixels" and are "localized in a small subregion", Sec. 1; I did not find a separate measurement of localisation, so UNVERIFIED beyond the figures). On context, verbatim (Sec. 3.2): "Their internal feature representations depend on the context and the same perturbation may not transfer between contexts." And Sec. 5 (Limitations): "how Tesseract recognizes a particular word depends on the words surrounding it." Hence word-level adversarial crops cannot be pasted into another document, and letter-level perturbations "do not transfer from word to word". No transfer to legacy Tesseract, GOCR, or OCRopus (binarisation "destroys almost the entire adversarial perturbation", Sec. 5).
- Limits (stated): transferability across contexts and across OCR models; physical realisability (Fig. 8 one example; scanned noise raises rejection, L2 14.3 vs about 2.9 digitally); Tesseract rejects slightly noisy inputs, which limits the attack space; larger alphabet (110 vs 26 for speech) makes targeted attacks harder (Sec. 7). (mine): context dependence is the authors' explanation for non-transfer, but non-transfer has other causes (position, rendering, preprocessing), and they did not run a controlled test, such as the same perturbation in two neighbouring-word contexts, to separate them.
- Relevance: It is the closest published analogue of a kraken model (conv + stacked LSTM + CTC, open weights) and shows adversarial behaviour probing on it, but its context claim is architecture-based and untested, so a weight-space intervention that edits specific recurrent units and checks cross-context transfer would test what J3 only asserts.
