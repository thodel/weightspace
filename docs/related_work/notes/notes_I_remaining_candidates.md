## I1. Machine unlearning for handwritten text recognition (closest prior art to editing a trained HTR model)
- Full text: arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08616 (PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.08616, v2 of 2025-10-14; I read v2 via pdftotext). No HAL copy checked.
- Authors: Lei Kang, Xuanshuo Fu, Lluis Gomez, Alicia Fornes, Ernest Valveny, Dimosthenis Karatzas. Pattern Recognition 2025 per the task; Crossref gives vol. 172, article 112411, issued 2026-04 (issue date). arXiv comment: "Accepted for publication in Pattern Recognition". DOI 10.1016/j.patcog.2025.112411. Exact title on arXiv: "Preserving Privacy Without Compromising Accuracy: Machine Unlearning for Handwritten Text Recognition".
- Model analysed or changed: the authors' own encoder-only transformer HTR baseline ("adopting the transformer architecture from the T5 encoder", trained from scratch, PyTorch), CNN front end, linear recognition head Hr; the multi-head variant adds a [CLS] token and a writer-classification head Hw. Word-level, not line-level; I found no occurrence of "CTC" in the text (recognition loss is called "sequence-recognition loss" L_rec; its exact form is UNVERIFIED). Not a kraken / VGSL / BiLSTM model.
- Question asked: can writer-identifying information be removed from a trained HTR model on request ("right to be forgotten") without full retraining and without losing recognition accuracy. RQ1: does a trained HTR model memorise user-identifiable information?
- Method (what is edited): a two-stage edit of the trained weights.
  - Stage I, neural pruning: run retain-set and forget-set images through the trained model, take layer activations, score each neuron by ratio S_forget/S_retain (+eps), zero the top K% per layer. Quote: "Neural weights are selectively set to zero based on our proposed importance score to remove inherent knowledge of the forget set."
  - Stage II, post-hoc unlearning by further gradient training (fine-tuning with a modified loss), not gradient ascent: Random Labeling (permuted writer labels on the forget set + beta*L_rec), Fisher Forgetting, Amnesiac Unlearning, DELETE distillation, plus the authors' Writer-ID Confusion (WIC): CE on writer labels for retain samples, KL(softmax(writer logits) || uniform) on forget samples, total loss L_WIC + beta*L_rec. The unlearning acts on the writer head; the recognition head is kept by the L_rec term.
  - Controls / comparisons: five unlearning methods in the same prune-unlearn pipeline, 2,000 vs 10,000 iterations, retain set and unseen test set as references, MANOVA on (ACC, CER, WER) for significance.
- Evidence the method does what it claims: (a) writer-ID accuracy on forget set (0.00 after WIC, Table 16, vs 100.00 baseline); (b) membership inference attack (3-layer MLP on recognition-head logits; retain = members, test = non-members): forget-set MIA success "49% and 48%" after 2,000 / 10,000 iterations vs 72.85% for the single-head baseline (Table 2) and 50% chance; (c) CER/WER on forget / retain / test sets. Grad-CAM is used only to argue that CNN features are low-level and similar across forget/retain/test (Fig. 4), a qualitative argument.
- Main finding (IAM, RWTH partition, words, 40,977 train words; Table 16): baseline forget/retain/test CER 1.80 / 1.29 / 13.23; pruning alone wrecks the model (pruned M*, 17.45% sparsity: CER 58.29 forget, 60.44 retain, 66.81 test); Prune-WIC (10,000 it., 7.42% sparsity) gives forget CER 0.97, retain 1.75, test 14.83, writer ACC on forget 0.00, "state-of-the-art or superior" trade-off versus the four other methods. Also evaluated on CVL (Section 4.6.7; not read in detail).
- Limits (stated): "While our work primarily focuses on writer identity, it does not yet address other sources of information leakage such as rare lexical patterns". Other stated directions: layout-aware and multilingual unlearning, DocVQA.
- Limits (mine): only IAM English word images (alphabet restricted to A-Za-z), one architecture trained from scratch; the edited quantity is writer identity (a style attribute) read out by an added classification head, not text content or a language prior; the writer head is a probe the authors add themselves, so "unlearning" is measured against that probe plus an MIA. The forget-set CER dropping to 0.97 means the edit leaves content recognition intact, which is the property a weight-space text edit would need to avoid. The relation between pruned sparsity (17.45% for M*) and the 7.4% sparsity in the final rows is not explained in the parts I read (UNVERIFIED).
- Relevance: it is the one paper in the batch that actually edits the weights of a trained HTR network (zeroing neurons selected by forget/retain activation ratio, then loss-guided fine-tuning), so it is the nearest precedent for activation-contrast neuron selection plus tuning, but it targets writer identity on a transformer word recogniser, not text content in a CTC/BiLSTM line recogniser.

## I2. Adversarial attacks on online handwriting, salience-based temporal editing
- Full text: arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12500 (PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12500, v2 2026-09-19; read via pdftotext).
- Authors: Yataro Tamura, Brian Kenji Iwana, Jiseok Lee. Year 2026 (first posted 2026-07-14). Venue: arXiv comment "Accepted at ICDAR 2026". DOI: none given. Code: "AITE" at github.com/yataro0117/AITE (stated as to be released).
- Model analysed: classifiers on online pen trajectories: 1D CNN-3 (surrogate, also 1D CNN-4 target), 2-layer BLSTM-2, encoder-only Transformer; Unipen (1A digits, 1B upper, 1C lower; T normalised to 50 steps) and CASIA-OLHWDB 1.1 (3,755 Chinese classes). Isolated single-character classification, not line recognition, no CTC.
- Question asked: can adversarial examples for online handwriting be made by editing the time axis (insert/delete points) rather than adding spatial noise, so that strokes stay smooth and transfer better in one-shot black-box settings?
- Method: Adversarial Iterative Temporal Editing (AITE). Salience is Grad-CAM adapted to 1D: channel weights alpha_k = mean over time of d z_c / d A_k (z_c = pre-softmax logit of class c, A = intermediate feature map), s(t) = sum_k alpha_k A_k(t), without the ReLU (so negative contributions are kept), then linear interpolation to input length T. Use: insert a midpoint into the interval with the lowest average salience for the original class; delete the point (endpoints excluded) with the highest salience. Quote: "Temporal salience is estimated using gradient-based activation mapping, which guides edits toward time steps that strongly support the original class prediction."
- Evidence the method measures what it claims: an ablation replacing Grad-CAM salience with gradient magnitude (||grad_x L||_2) per time step (Section 5.6, Table 3): gradient-based selection gives more similar samples but weaker attacks in both white-box and black-box. Also visual similarity measures (Section 5.5). No evidence stated that Grad-CAM salience matches human or causal importance beyond attack success.
- Main finding: image-style additive attacks are strong in white-box but transfer poorly; temporal editing gives stronger one-shot black-box transfer while preserving shape and smoothness (abstract; I did not copy the numbers).
- Limits (stated): preliminary; future work to extend "to word- and sentence-level online handwriting recognition"; no human evaluation of naturalness; defences not studied.
- Limits (mine): attack surrogate and targets are small classifiers; Grad-CAM needs a single class logit, which a CTC line model does not have (would need a per-frame or per-character target).
- Relevance: a salience (Grad-CAM) map used to decide which input positions to alter is an input-side perturbation of what the network reads, and shows the gradient-vs-Grad-CAM contrast; it does not edit weights and is on isolated characters, so it is useful only as an input-perturbation baseline for weight-space probes.

## I3. Label-Context-Dependent Internal Language Model Estimation for CTC
- Full text: arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06096 (PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06096, v1 2025-06-06; read via pdftotext).
- Authors: Zijian Yang, Minh-Nghia Phan, Ralf Schlueter, Hermann Ney (RWTH Aachen; AppTek). Interspeech 2025; Crossref DOI 10.21437/interspeech.2025-1378, pp. 5193-5197. Code: github.com/rwth-i6/returnn-experiments (2025-label-dependent-ILM-ctc). Speech, not HTR.
- Model analysed: 12-layer Conformer CTC acoustic model (BPE 10k), Librispeech 960 h (in-domain), TED-LIUM 2 (cross-domain). ILM estimator: a one-layer LSTM LM (embedding 128, hidden 1000).
- Question asked: although CTC assumes label-context independence, does it implicitly learn a context-dependent internal LM, and how can that ILM be estimated for ILM-correction in decoding with an external LM?
- Method (estimated, not edited; CTC is frozen): knowledge distillation with CTC as teacher and an autoregressive LM as student. Label posteriors P(a | a_1^{s-1}, X) are obtained from CTC prefix probabilities, <EOS> posterior from the full-sequence probability; training criterion = KL between the CTC label posterior and the student, on the CTC training data. Because the teacher is overconfident on seen data, two regularisers: label-level KD with smoothing (interpolate joint empirical distribution with product of marginals, within mini-batch; alpha = 0.5) and with masking (mask the acoustic frames of a label with probability 0.4, using GMM alignment boundaries). Also sequence-level KD. Baselines: frame-level unigram prior (FP), transcription LM, shallow fusion.
- Evidence it estimates the ILM: indirect, via decoding WER with ILM correction. Quote: "context-dependent ILMs outperform the context-independent priors in cross-domain evaluation, indicating that CTC learns a context-dependent ILM". The authors note ILM perplexity does not correlate with WER ("new metrics need to be explored to better evaluate ILMs"), and checkpoints were chosen on dev WER. Unregularised label KD behaves like a transcription LM.
- Main finding: cross-domain (TED-LIUM 2), shallow fusion 14.3/15.9 dev/test WER vs 11.9/13.8 with label-level KD+smoothing; the paper states "more than 13%" relative improvement over shallow fusion. In-domain all ILM methods perform about equally (Librispeech). Inconsistency (mine): abstract and Section 4.2 say "with smoothing" is best, the conclusion says "with sampling".
- Limits (stated): the optimal context length "requires further investigation"; assumption that the CTC ILM is weak (hence small LSTM); ILM PPL is not a usable selection criterion.
- Limits (mine): speech, BPE units, Conformer; the estimate is a separate LM distilled from output posteriors, so it measures what the CTC outputs imply about label context, not where that knowledge sits in the weights. Needs the training data to run.
- Relevance: gives a concrete, model-agnostic recipe to quantify the "implicit language model" of a frozen CTC network (distil prefix-conditional posteriors into a small LM, compare to context-independent priors), which could be applied to kraken CTC outputs before and after a weight edit.

## I4. Separating optical and language models through encoder-decoder strategy
- Full text: HAL https://hal.science/hal-01821598 (PDF https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01821598/file/icfhr%282%29.pdf; read via pdftotext).
- Title as printed: "Separating Optical and Language Models through Encoder-Decoder Strategy for Transferable Handwriting Recognition" (the task title says "Handwritten Text Recognition"; the source says "Handwriting"). Authors: Adeline Granet, Emmanuel Morin, Harold Mouchere, Solen Quiniou, Christian Viard-Gaudin (LS2N, Nantes). 16th ICFHR, Aug 2018, Niagara Falls, Canada. DOI 10.1109/icfhr-2018.2018.00061 (per HAL citation). Pages 309-314 per a search result (UNVERIFIED).
- Model changed: an optical encoder (7 conv layers, 3x3, 100 px height, six position masks, 2 dense layers of 1,024-2,048 units) maps a word image to a vector of letter n-grams (1-3 grams, about 12,500 selected); a separate decoder generates the character sequence from that vector. Trained separately (decoder architecture details not checked).
- Question asked: can optical and language knowledge be separated so the optical part transfers across languages sharing an alphabet (GW English, RIMES French -> Esposalles Spanish) when the target has no ground truth?
- Method: encoder outputs normalised letter-n-gram frequencies (with a +0.5 offset target, sigmoid/binary cross-entropy vs KL variants); decoder trained only on text resources (VESP, Google Books, Wikipedia, GW and RIMES vocabularies). Evaluated by n-gram recall/precision and word/character recognition rates. No CTC (CTC is mentioned only as state of the art in the introduction).
- Evidence it separates the two: none beyond transfer results. With same-source data recall exceeds 70%; transfer from GW/RIMES to ESP reaches only about half of the in-domain measures ("it seems that the amount of data used for the training step is still too low").
- Main finding: quote: "Experiments show that separating optical and language model can be a solution for multilingual transfer learning." (abstract; the body is more cautious).
- Limits (stated): too little training data for the encoder; the n-gram vector loses character order; future work: attention, limiting active n-grams. (mine) Word-level images only; the "language model" is an explicit decoder here, not an implicit one inside a CTC net.
- Relevance: an architecture-level (by-design) separation of optical and linguistic parts, the opposite of editing them apart in an existing CTC network, so it is a contrast case for claims that the two can be separated in weight space.

- Relevance: supplies a cheap output-side taxonomy (substitution pairs, special-character errors) usable to describe how a weight edit changes errors of a CTC recogniser, but contains no weight-level analysis.

## I8. CER-TPR: Quantifying Text-Context Bias in VLM-Based OCR
- Full text: abstract only (SSRN landing page not fetched; abstract from the SSRN record via Crossref https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7459957; OpenAlex says "green" OA but gives no PDF URL).
- Authors: Min-Seok Yang, Ju-Hyeon Seong. 2026, SSRN preprint, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.7459957. A second SSRN record with a different title and abstract exists: "CER-TPR: A Novel Metric for Quantifying Text-Context Bias in Pretrained VLM-Based OCR", DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6597744 (same authors, 2026), apparently an earlier version (mine).
- Models analysed: four VLMs (named in the abstract: Qwen3-VL 32B, Mistral Small 3.2; the other two not named in the abstract) on cropped line- and word-level OCR; datasets: a maritime document dataset ("Dataset A") and IIIT5K-Words ("Dataset B").
- Question: when a VLM gets an image plus an external text hint, does it follow the image or the text; when do correct hints help and wrong hints hurt?
- Method: input-side manipulation of context. Hints = realistic external OCR output, ground truth, random text, and independently generated one-edit and two-edit character corruptions (a "conflict-conditioned evaluation"). Metrics: Exact Text Preference Ratio (Exact TPR), CER-based TPR (CER-TPR, character-level directional attribution for partially modified answers), Preference Margin, Correction Rate, Error Induction Rate. Ground-truth and random hints serve as controls. TPR is derived from the "blind faith in text" line of work (arXiv 2503.02199 per a search snippet; not checked).
- Evidence the metric works: CER-TPR gives a directional label for "63.0% on Dataset A and 79.2% on Dataset B" of responses that Exact TPR leaves unresolved (abstract numbers).
- Main finding: external context improves aggregate EM and CER for all four VLMs, but wrong hints induce model- and dataset-dependent errors (Qwen3-VL 32B Correction Rate 0.9255; Mistral Small 3.2 Error Induction Rate 0.5346 on Dataset A). Quote: "OCR accuracy and source preference should be evaluated separately."
- Limits: not available at abstract level. (mine) Hints are placed in the prompt, so this measures reliance on prompt text, not a language prior stored in weights.
- Relevance: a ready-made protocol (clean / correct / wrong / corrupted text context, followed by character-level directional attribution) to test whether a weight edit or a QLoRA adapter changes how a VLM recogniser trades image against text.

## I9. Text Pixels Are Not Enough: A Controlled Study of Canvas Sensitivity in VLM-Based OCR
- Full text: abstract only. Springer page https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-36207-0_6 shows the abstract and a paywall ("preview of subscription content"); no arXiv or author copy found (arXiv API title/author/abstract searches empty).

- Models analysed: four VLMs (GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen 3.5-27B, InternVL 3.5-8B) and four OCR systems, all unmodified.
- Question: should OCR predictions stay stable when only the surrounding canvas size changes and the text pixels stay the same?

- Limits: not stated at abstract level; (mine) synthetic benchmark; the cause is inferred, no internal analysis.
- Relevance: a controlled input-perturbation design (change one factor, hold text pixels fixed) that a weight-space study can borrow to separate effects of an edit from effects of image geometry.

## I10. Investigating OCR-Sensitive Neurons to Improve Entity Recognition in Historical Documents
- Full text: arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16934 (PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.16934, v3 2024-11-18; read via pdftotext).
- Authors: Emanuela Boros, Maud Ehrmann (EPFL DHLAB). Published version per Crossref: Lecture Notes in Computer Science, in "Sustainability and Empowerment in the Context of Digital Libraries", 2024-12-06, DOI 10.1007/978-981-96-0865-2_5 (conference name not given by Crossref; UNVERIFIED).
- Confirmation of the question: YES, the analysed network is a downstream LM, not an OCR recogniser. Models: Llama2-7B and Mistral-7B (decoder-only transformers, 32 blocks), analysed on MLP up-projection layers. The "OCR noise" is synthetic: 158,513 correct French nouns/verbs from corrected historical newspapers (PleIAs Post-OCR-Correction), each altered three times with NLPAug at low / average / high character-distortion levels. No OCR engine is analysed.
- Question asked: are there layers/neurons in an LLM that react to OCR noise, and does neutralising them improve NER on noisy historical text?
- Method: (1) CKA between layer activations for correct vs altered tokens to find sensitive layer regions; (2) per-neuron activation differences (mean over pairs); a neuron is "OCR-sensitive" if its difference exceeds mean + 1 std of the layer in more than 90% of pairs; (3) neuron neutralisation: multiply selected MLP up-projection activations by a mask with factor alpha (0.1, 0.5, 0.9; 100 to 11,000 neurons per layer in steps of 100) during the forward pass of test data; models LoRA-fine-tuned for NER on HIPE-2020 and AJMC (French), five runs averaged. Controls: baseline fine-tuning without ablation; multiple alpha and bin sizes.
- Evidence the neurons are OCR-specific: indirect (F1 change after neutralisation; CKA/neuron counts agree only partly). The authors say CKA and neuron counts show an inverse relation "albeit with some exceptions" and that it "remains difficult to obtain unambiguous observations".
- Main finding: neutralising a few neurons gives up to about 3% (Llama2) and 5% (Mistral) relative F1 gain on AJMC; HIPE-2020 needs larger bins (>6,400) in middle and late layers. Baseline F1 e.g. 50.72 (Llama2), 52.88 (Mistral) on HIPE-2020.
- Limits (stated): two French datasets only; only nouns and verbs; scaling-down is a simplistic edit that ignores compensatory mechanisms; practical deployment (dynamically identifying neurons) is hard.
- Limits (mine): selection of the neuron set to neutralise is partly by sweeping on the test data, so gains are not clean held-out estimates; the work is a forward-pass activation edit, not a permanent weight change.
- Relevance: a direct template for "find units by clean-vs-perturbed activation contrast, then scale them in the forward pass and measure downstream change", applicable to BiLSTM/conv units of a CTC recogniser (here applied to a text model fed noisy OCR output, so it says nothing about the recogniser itself).

## I12. Optical modelling and language modelling trade-off for Handwritten Text Recognition
- Full text: abstract only (IEEE paywalled; OpenAlex closed, no repository copy; searched RiuNet UPV, nothing). Abstract from the UPV Explora record https://aplicat.upv.es/exploraupv/ficha-publicacion/publicacion/293219 and OpenAlex.

- Model analysed: the HTR system's optical models and language model, reused across collections (system type UNVERIFIED from abstract).
- Question: when reusing models trained on a different collection, what do the language model and the optical models each contribute to performance?
- Method (abstract): empirical evaluation training on Jeremy Bentham manuscripts to recognise a manuscript on a very different topic written by Jane Austen. Detail of controls UNVERIFIED.
- Evidence: not available at abstract level.
- Main finding: not stated in the abstract. Quote: "focusing on the contribution that the language model and the optical models have on the performance".
- Limits: cannot judge. (mine) Two collections only per the abstract.
- Relevance: an early, explicit separation of optical versus language contributions by swapping models between collections, i.e. an external-component analogue of the question of how much a CTC net's behaviour is optical versus linguistic.
